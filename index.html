Glossary of Terms

Udasi
A journey or pilgrimage, specifically referring to the five major journeys of Guru Nanak Dev Ji.
Janamsakhi
Biographical literature detailing the life of Guru Nanak Dev Ji.
Shabad
A hymn or sacred poem from the Guru Granth Sahib.
Gurdwara
A Sikh place of worship and community center.
Langar
The free community kitchen served at Gurdwaras, open to all visitors regardless of background.
Sat Sang
True congregation or holy company, referring to spiritual fellowship.

Udasi II

South India & Sri Lanka (1506 – 1513)

A long voyage through southern kingdoms and across the sea to Sri Lanka (Ceylon/Singladip), engaging with temple cultures and Dravidian spiritual schools.

Traditional

Kanchipuram & Srirangam

Tamil Nadu, India | Southern Temple Centers

Traveled south through the Deccan plateau. Visited the historic Shiva and Vishnu temple centers of Kanchipuram and Srirangam. Discussed theological concepts of idol worship, divine immanence, and devotion (Bhakti) with local Vaishnava and Shaiva priests.

Source: Fauja Singh, Atlas: Travels [3]
Traditional

Rameshwaram & Setubandha

Tamil Nadu, India | Southern Coast

Reached the southern tip of India. Before embarking on a ship across the Palk Strait to Ceylon, he visited Rameshwaram, a holy site linked to the Ramayana. Spoke about the non-dualistic nature of God, who transcends physical temple boundaries.

Source: Puratan Janamsakhi [5]
Disputed

Sri Lanka (Singladip)

Sri Lanka | Kingdom of Shivnabh

Crossed the sea to Ceylon, traditionally called Singladip in Sikh texts. He was received by King Shivnabh. The king, seeking proof of spiritual authority, tested the Guru. Moved by Nanak's spiritual wisdom and simple presence, he and his court became followers, establishing a localized congregation (Dharamsala).

"The mind is the king; the mind is the priest; the mind is the path of salvation." — Guru Nanak (Maru Solahe)
Source: Puratan Janamsakhi, Gurinder Singh Mann [2] [5]
Traditional

Ujjain

Madhya Pradesh, India | Central Return Route

On the return journey northward, he visited Ujjain along the Shipra River. Visited the Bharthari Caves and debated with Nath Yogis. He argued that yoga is not about physical breath controls or yogic postures, but inner alignment with truth and compassionate action.

Source: Atlas: Travels of Guru Nanak [3]
Traditional

Somnath & Gujarat

Gujarat, India | Western Coast

Visited the famous Somnath temple. Addressed the priests on the futility of guarding external stone idols while failing to defend the inner temple of the human heart from anger, lust, and greed.

Source: Janamsakhi traditions [5]
⚜
Udasi III

Himalayas & the North (1514 – 1518)

An arduous ascent into the Himalayan peaks, Kashmir, Ladakh, and trans-Himalayan Tibet to challenge extreme asceticism and preach active social responsibility.

Traditional

Kangra, Mandi & Kullu

Himachal Pradesh, India | Himalayan Foothills

Traveled through the steep valleys of Himachal. Visited ancient temple sites like Jawalaji (glowing volcanic fire temple) and Baijnath. Met local rulers and ascetics, highlighting that escaping to isolated mountains does not solve spiritual ignorance.

Source: Atlas: Travels of Guru Nanak [3]
Traditional

Srinagar

Kashmir, India | Valley of Scholars

In Kashmir, he met the renowned Kashmiri Pandit Brahm Das, who arrived carrying loads of Sanskrit scriptures to display his intellectual superiority. Guru Nanak pointed out that reading books without internalizing love and humility is mere pride. Brahm Das renounced his pride and became a disciple.

"One may read thousands of books, one may read cartloads of books... but only one word matters: the word of Divine Love." — Guru Nanak (Asa di Var)
Source: Puratan Janamsakhi [5]
Traditional

Ladakh

Jammu & Kashmir, India | Trans-Himalayan Region

Traveled to high altitudes, meeting Tibetan Buddhist monks (Lamas). Local folklore remembers him as *Nanak Lama* or *Rinpoche Nanak*. A famous site, Gurdwara Pathar Sahib, commemorates an encounter where a local tyrant tried to crush the Guru with a boulder, which miraculously turned to wax upon touching him.

Source: Local Ladakhi folklore & traditions [5]
Debated Location Disputed

Sumer Parbat (Mount Kailash)

Tibet | Sacred Trans-Himalayan Peak

Traditional accounts locate his dialogue with the Siddh Yogis (disciples of Gorakhnath) on "Sumer Parbat" (traditionally identified with Mt. Kailash/Manasarovar). The Yogis asked how the plains of Punjab were faring. Nanak replied that when the wise men flee to live in mountain caves, the common people are left in darkness. This dialogue is recorded in the Guru Granth Sahib as the *Siddh Gosht*.

"How can the world cross the ocean of existence? As a lotus floats untouched upon water, as a duck floats on a stream... so does one cross the world by keeping the mind anchored in the Shabad." — Guru Nanak (Siddh Gosht, Raag Ramkali)
Source: Siddh Gosht, Puratan Janamsakhi [5]
Traditional

Achal Batala

Punjab, India | Return Route

Upon returning to Punjab, he attended the Shivratri fair at Achal Batala. Had a famous discourse with Yogi Bhangar Nath, who accused the Guru of mixing household life with spiritual pursuits (like adding sour yeast to milk). Nanak replied that the Yogis themselves beg for food from the very householders they look down upon.

Source: Bhai Gurdas (Var 1, Pauri 39-44) [4]
⚜
Udasi IV

West Asia & Middle East (1519 – 1524)

His final and most international journey: dressing in pilgrim garb, crossing Sindh, and traveling to the Islamic holy cities of Mecca, Medina, and Baghdad.

Traditional

Lakhpat & Hinglaj

Sindh & Balochistan, Pakistan | Gateway to Gulf

Traveled southwest into Sindh. Visited Lakhpat (Kutch, Gujarat) and the sacred cave temple of Hinglaj Devi in Balochistan. Adopted a composite attire—wearing a blue robe resembling Sufi dervishes and carrying a staff, symbolizing a traveler seeking universal truth.

Source: Fauja Singh, Atlas: Travels [3]
Sacred Center Documented

Mecca

Saudi Arabia | Holy City of Islam

Traveled by ship to Jeddah and walked to Mecca. Tired from his journey, he fell asleep with his feet pointing toward the holy Kaaba. A religious official, Jiwan, kicked him in anger, demanding: "How dare you point your feet toward the House of God?" Nanak replied calmly: "Brother, please turn my feet in the direction where God does not reside." Jiwan turned his feet, only to realize the divine presence is everywhere.

"The Muslim says: 'He is in Mecca'; the Hindu says: 'He is in the East'. But Nanak says: He resides in every heart, in all directions." — Bhai Gurdas (Var 1, Pauri 32)
Source: Bhai Gurdas, Puratan Janamsakhi [4] [5]
Sacred Center Disputed

Medina

Saudi Arabia | City of the Prophet

Traditional narratives describe Nanak traveling to Medina, engaging in discussions with Islamic theologians on the spiritual essence of prayer, fasting, and submission to the Divine Will (Hukam).

Source: Puratan Janamsakhi, Encyclopaedia Britannica [1] [5]
Interfaith Dialogue Documented

Baghdad

Iraq | Center of Sufism

Traveled north to Baghdad, a historic capital of Islamic learning. Sat outside the city and raised a loud call to prayer (*Azan*), but modified it to praise the infinite universes. Met with the local Sufi leader, Pir Bahlol. A stone inscription dating to 917 Hijri (1511-1521 CE) was discovered in Baghdad commemorating the visit of "Baba Nanak Fakir."

Source: Bhai Gurdas (Var 1), Archeological Stone Inscription [4]
Traditional

Kabul & Peshawar

Afghanistan & Khyber Pass | Return Route

Returned via Kabul and Peshawar (Gorakhhatri temple). Engaged in dialogues with Sufi and yogic institutions in Peshawar. Taught local communities to earn an honest living (Kirat Karni) and share their earnings (Vand Chhakna).

Source: Gurinder Singh Mann [2]
Traditional

Hasan Abdal (Panja Sahib)

Punjab, Pakistan

Settled near a hill where a Muslim dervish, Wali Qandhari, lived. Seeking water for his companion Mardana, the Guru asked Wali Qandhari, who refused out of pride. The Guru dug a stone at the base, and water bubbled up, while Wali Qandhari's spring dried up. Enraged, Wali Qandhari rolled a massive boulder down the hill. Guru Nanak stopped it with his open hand, leaving an imprint on the stone—now commemorated as Gurdwara Panja Sahib.

Source: Janamsakhi traditions [5]
Culmination of Udasis Documented

Kartarpur

Punjab, Pakistan | The Sacred Commune

After 24 years of travel, Guru Nanak settled on the banks of the Ravi River. He took off his traveler robes, put on the clothes of a farmer, and established a self-sustaining agricultural community. Here he instituted the practice of *Langar* (free communal kitchen, where king and commoner sat side-by-side to eat) and nominated his successor Bhai Lehna (Guru Angad Dev Ji), laying the institutional foundation for the Sikh faith.

"Truth is the highest virtue, but higher still is truthful living." — Guru Nanak (Raag Sri Rag)
Source: Puratan & Bala Janamsakhis, Gurinder Singh Mann [2] [5]

Academic Bibliography & Sources

This digital interactive mapping resource is built upon historical Sikh traditions and academic publications:

  1. Encyclopaedia Britannica: "Guru Nanak" (written by William Hewat McLeod). Summarizes the limits of Janamsakhi narratives as historical evidence, noting they contain hagiographical components written decades after the Guru's passing.
  2. Gurinder Singh Mann: "Guru Nanak's Life and Legacy: An Appraisal" (Journal of Punjab Studies, Vol. 17). Highlights the reconstruction of his life through the Puratan Janamsakhi framework and early Sikh manuscripts.
  3. Fauja Singh & Kirpal Singh: "Atlas: Travels of Guru Nanak" (Punjabi University Patiala, 1976). Provides geographic mapping by contrasting different traditional streams (Bala, Puratan, Meharban) and outlining the best historical inferences for routes.
  4. Bhai Gurdas's Vars (Var 1): The earliest written records (c. 1600) detailing the travels to Baghdad, Mecca, and dialogues with Siddhs.
  5. Janamsakhi Traditions: Puratan & Bala Manuscripts. Traditional narrative accounts of Guru Nanak's life, documenting oral histories and early communal memory.
Cultural Sensitivity & Historical Debates: Sikh tradition holds the journeys to be spiritually guided missionary work spanning the breadth of the known world. In academic circles, debates exist regarding the exact sequence, the historicity of specific distant locations (such as Turkey or parts of Sri Lanka), and the translation of place-names (e.g. identifying Sumer Parbat with Mount Kailash vs. sites in Himachal Pradesh). This website respects both the deep spiritual significance of these sites to millions of devotees and the rigorous standards of modern historical analysis.
